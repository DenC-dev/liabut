Project Liabut

How do Timor’s Austronesian languages branch?

Two classifications of the Austronesian languages of Timor disagree. Geoffrey Hull puts Lakalei and Idaté with Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede. Charles Grimes and Owen Edwards make Mambae, Kemak, Tokodede and Welaun a branch of their own and put Lakalei and Idaté with the languages to the east. Liabut asks what the basic vocabulary of 24 languages says, starting from a protocol frozen before any analysis.

In brief: the vocabulary puts Lakalei-Idate beside Central Timor and never with the eastern languages, and outgroups place the root outside that pair. But the placement is fragile, its shared innovations no longer favour Central Timor once retentions are removed, and the sound change that defines Timor-Babar points the other way. By a rule declared before the last round, the question stays unresolved. Welaun fits Central Timor once borrowable words are set aside.

What the vocabulary says

Two questions divide the classifications. C1: does Lakalei-Idate belong with Central Timor, as in Hull, or with Timor-Babar, as in Grimes and Edwards (2026)? C2: is Welaun a dialect of Tetun, as Hull (1998) first had it, or a Central Timor language, as Edwards (2019) argued?

  • Lakalei-Idate is unresolved by the vocabulary, with no lean. The trees place it beside Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede and never with the eastern languages, and seven outgroups beyond Flores put the root on the western side, outside that pair. Against that, kept together it reaches only 70% with Central Timor on the main lists, Idaté alone leans partly towards its neighbour Habu, and once identified retentions are removed its shared innovations tie or lose against Habu. If lenition cannot reverse, *p lenition needs six separate origins on the lexical tree and two on Grimes and Edwards’s. After correction no AU topology test rejects any placement, though a direct Bayesian comparison favours Central Timor over the eastern placement (log Bayes factor +11.8).
  • Welaun fits Central Timor once borrowable words are set aside. The model-based trees put it there (posterior 0.86; log Bayes factor +7.6 against Welaun + Tetun) and its Tetun look sits in borrowable vocabulary. Distance trees and a strict consensus coding put it with Tetun, and the protocol said in advance that this question can’t be settled from vocabulary alone.
  • Fabronic, Timor-Babar and Eastern Timor get no lexical support, under five cognate codings and on independent lists. That says little about Timor-Babar: the same trees miss about half of the subgroups that sound changes define and nobody disputes.
  • The low-level groups hold. Lakalei-Idate, Kawaimina, Wetar-Atauro, Nuclear Rote, Kisar-Luang and Babar all pass the bar.
  • The pre-registered rule returned “unresolved” for both questions, and the two replications with predictions stated in advance failed against Tetun. A mock referee report then prompted a second round, declared before it was run, with its own decision rule; it returned “unresolved” again.

Ramelaic. Hull’s group of Mambae, Kemak, Tokodede and Idalaka, his name for Idaté and Lakalei together.

Timor-Babar. Grimes and Edwards’s branch for every Austronesian language of Timor outside Central Timor, defined by the change of *p to *h.

Lakalei-Idate: where does it go?

Lakalei and Idaté, spoken in central Timor-Leste, carry the whole disagreement. Their vocabulary puts them beside Central Timor, while their sound changes look eastern: they have *p lenition and *ŋ > n, and lack Central Timor’s *ŋ > g, k. Two questions follow. Where is the root of the tree, since “beside Central Timor” and “first branch of Timor-Babar” can be the same tree rooted in two places? And how much of the Central Timor signal is inheritance rather than shared retention or contact?

Posterior probability. The share of sampled trees that contain a group.

Outgroup. A language known to sit outside the group being studied, used to find the root.

Where the root falls

The two Flores lects first used as the root don’t sit outside Timor: they attach to Rote-Meto and Helong. So seven more outgroups were coded together with the Timor data: Tagalog, Buginese and Uma, three Central Malayo-Polynesian languages from Sumba, Sumbawa and western Flores, and the reconstructed Proto-Malayo-Polynesian words. A relaxed clock gives a root without outgroups.

Rooted clade. A group that excludes every outgroup, so that it really descends from one common ancestor within the tree.

In none of these does the root fall on the Central Timor stem, where Grimes and Edwards’s tree would need it, so Lakalei-Idate and Central Timor end up together on one side. That answers the rooting worry but doesn’t settle the question: outgroups attach by lexical similarity too, and Rote-Meto’s place next to the root could reflect conservatism or contact across the Savu Sea.

Kept together, and taken apart

Both classifications treat Lakalei-Idate as one unit, but the trees don’t always keep it together. Held together, it joins Central Timor at 70% on the main lists and 98% on lists collected independently of Grimes and Edwards. Taken apart, Lakalei is Central Timor-like every time (84% and 99%), while Idaté leans partly towards Habu, its neighbour 5 to 11 km away (38% on the main lists). Removing Tetun raises the Central Timor link to 94%; removing the 132 cognate sets shared with Papuan languages halves it, because Mambae, Kemak, Idaté and Lakalei share more words with their Papuan neighbours than the eastern languages do. No coding or subset ever puts Lakalei-Idate with the eastern group.

Shared replacements, cleaned and polarised

The triplet test counts concepts on which exactly two of three groups share a word that replaced the Proto-Malayo-Polynesian one. Of the six replacements Lakalei-Idate shares only with Central Timor, review found four to be errors: two ‘wife’ sets continue one old word, ‘who’ pairs unrelated forms, and ‘dry’ is doubtful. That leaves ‘cry’ sɛro and ‘spit’, against nine shared only with eastern groups, which have been checked only automatically. Once 66 identified retentions are removed and words also found in Flores, Rote-Meto or Wetar are set aside, the pairing with Central Timor still beats Tetun and Kawaimina on stable vocabulary but loses to Habu (10 against 12).

The check. Welaun’s contact with Tetun is documented. The test finds it in borrowable words (Z = 6.9, beyond 99.8% of contact-free simulations) and not in stable ones (Z = 0.6).

Polarised. Counting only words absent from languages outside the triplet, so that an old shared word lost by one group isn’t mistaken for a new one.

Borrowing models and other views

Approximate Bayesian computation simulated 20,000 histories under each placement, with borrowing allowed from both sides. It can’t tell them apart: the random forest splits its votes 0.45 to 0.55 and misclassifies 36% of its own simulations, and the borrowing estimates (medians 22 to 25%) barely move from their prior (median 30%). Historical glottometry finds Lakalei-Idate cohesive on its own and tied about equally weakly to Central Timor (0.06, interval 0 to 0.31) and to the east (0.08, interval 0 to 0.41). A contact-aware tree capped at five transfer edges used all five in every sample, and its base tree puts Lakalei-Idate with Tetun and Habu, carrying the Central Timor resemblance through a transfer edge between Lakalei and Tokodede. The model is strained (some edges link unlikely partners), so this shows only that a few transfer events can explain the Central Timor signal as well as descent can.

Prior error. How often the method picks the wrong placement on its own simulated data. At 36%, it can barely tell the placements apart.

Sound changes

Grimes and Edwards’s comparative tables were turned into data: 257 tables and 14,401 forms, with the reflexes the authors mark. On the comparative method’s own terms, Lakalei-Idate sides with Timor-Babar. It keeps *p in 0 of 6 matched words, against 54 of 55 in Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede, and native words show the loss too: ‘four’ aːt, ‘tooth’ niɑn, and Grimes and Edwards’s ‘how much’ and ‘fire’. It also lacks Central Timor’s *ŋ > g (‘ear’ talina against Mambae teliga). Two facts limit these as markers. Welaun, Central Timor on every other count, has *p > h in native words, so the change happened more than once on Timor. And several changes recur outside their groups, in Sika, Dela, Galoli and Ili’uun.

Scored on the rival trees, the answer depends on whether a change can reverse. If lenition is irreversible, as historical linguists usually assume, *p lenition must arise six separate times on the lexical tree and only twice on Grimes and Edwards’s, which clearly favours theirs. The other ten characters are sparse and often parallel, and their totals point different ways under different scoring rules. The vocabulary and the phonology disagree, and that disagreement is the main finding here.

Reflex. The sound a language has where the proto-language had a given sound, as Tetun h where Proto-Malayo-Polynesian had *p.

Irreversible. A change like *p > h that is not undone later. Counting it that way, each separate appearance costs a step.

The second round

A mock referee report argued that the earlier “leans Central Timor” reading rested on an unrooted tree, on sound changes scored in a way that allowed reversals, and on shared “innovations” that included retentions. Each point became a test, declared with a binding decision rule before any was run. Central Timor would be reported as lexically supported only if all four conditions below held.

Holm correction. A way of keeping the chance of any false positive at 5% when many tests are run together.

Grimes and Edwards’s placement would have been reported as supported if Timor-Babar came out as a rooted clade, or if the root sat on the Central Timor stem. Neither happened. So the vocabulary leaves the question open. What it does show: no tree, coding or subset groups Lakalei-Idate with the eastern languages, and the outgroups put the root on the western side.

How it was done

  1. Data. Basic vocabulary from LexiRumah 3.0.1: 70 lects of the Timor region and 152 concepts, with 24 lects, one per language, in the model-based analyses. ABVD’s expert cognate judgements served as a benchmark. The Idaté and Lakalei lists come from surveys by Marian Klamer (2002) and Virginia Dawson (2014).
  2. Cognates. Automatic detection with LexStat in LingPy, scored against the experts at a B-cubed F of 0.81, and checked under five codings and their consensus. A gate of known low-level groups had to be recovered before any contested group was scored.
  3. Trees. Distance trees with 1,000 concept bootstraps, Bayesian trees in MrBayes with stepping-stone marginal likelihoods, maximum-likelihood trees and AU tests in IQ-TREE, a root from seven outgroups beyond Flores and from a relaxed clock.
  4. Sound changes. Grimes and Edwards’s tables as an expert reflex atlas, checked against an automatic atlas (94% agreement), coded as characters and scored on the rival trees with and without reversals.
  5. Inheritance or contact. Retention and innovation classes for every cognate set, validated against experts and cleaned by review; the triplet test, polarised and calibrated by simulation; a split test; approximate Bayesian computation with random forests; historical glottometry with resampling intervals; clock analyses; a contact-aware tree.

B-cubed F. A score from 0 to 1 for how well automatic cognate sets match expert ones. 0.81 is usable but below the 0.89 that List, Greenhill and Gray (2017) report across families, which is why the results are checked under several codings.

  2. 10 OctoberAddenda 1 and 2. The gate of known groups first failed on one group. A revised gate was adopted (post hoc), and further rules were fixed before any contested group was scored.
  3. 10 OctoberPre-registered tests run. Decision: unresolved for C1 and C2.
  4. 10 to 11 OctoberAddenda 3 and 4 (post hoc). Bayesian reruns, Grimes and Edwards’s tables as data, reflex atlases and total-evidence trees.
  5. 11 OctoberAddenda 5 and 6 (post hoc). Hull’s *b and *k as characters, an ASJP check, cognate-coding robustness and Alcantara’s (2015) Idaté list.
  6. 11 OctoberAddenda 7 and 8 (post hoc). Shared innovations, the triplet test, contact-aware trees, the split test, borrowing simulation, glottometry, clock dating and other branches.
  7. 11 OctoberAddendum 9. Ten checks on the first version’s weaknesses, two of them confirmatory, declared before any was run.
  8. 11 OctoberAddendum 10. An etymological check against the Austronesian Comparative Dictionary, and AU tests on the independent lists.
  9. 11 OctoberAddendum 11. The decisive words checked against Grimes and Edwards’s own tables, a parametric bootstrap, and a contact model capped at five transfer edges.
  10. 11 OctoberAddendum 12. A second round after a mock referee report: the root with seven outgroups, a relaxed clock, irreversible sound changes, Lakalei-Idate kept together, cleaned innovations, and a decision rule declared in advance. Verdict: unresolved.

What still limits the result

Little of this is confirmatory: the pre-registered rule returned “unresolved”, the replications failed against Tetun, and the second round’s rule returned “unresolved” again. Everything else is exploratory and was planned after earlier results had been seen. The cognates are automatic and only partly checked by hand. LexiRumah is a single compilation, although its Idaté, Lakalei and replacement Central Timor lists come from other collectors than Edwards and Grimes. Some sound-change cells rest on a handful of words. The simulated nulls use trees fitted to the same data. The outgroups attach by lexical similarity and could be pulled west by contact across the Savu Sea. Hull (1998) and Carvalho (2001) could not be obtained.

What would settle C1: a specialist’s review of the decisive cognate sets on both sides (the packets are ready), Grimes and Edwards’s full field lists for Idaté and Lakalei, Hull’s 1998 percentages, Alcantara’s complete Idaté corpus, and a regular-correspondence analysis of complete wordlists.

Use of AI. The code, the analyses, the drafting and the mock referee report were produced with substantial assistance from Claude (Anthropic), an AI system, under the author’s direction. Every analysis, decision and correction is recorded in the public repository so that it can be checked.
