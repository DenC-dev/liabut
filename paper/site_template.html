Where do Idaté and Lakalei belong?

Two classifications of Timor’s Austronesian languages disagree about two small languages of central Timor-Leste. Geoffrey Hull puts Lakalei and Idaté with Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede. Charles Grimes and Owen Edwards put them with the languages to the east. Liabut asks what their basic vocabulary says, under rules fixed before any analysis.

  • ShowsThe vocabulary trees place them beside Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede, but not robustly; the key sound change points the other way.
  • SuggestsA history shaped by contact or a dialect chain as well as descent.
  • UnknownWhich classification is right. That needs specialists and fuller word lists.

The disagreement

Two family trees for the same languages. Switch between them: only Lakalei and Idaté, and Welaun, change places.

Languages that descend from a common ancestor form a family tree. Linguists draw its branches from changes that one group of languages went through together and no other did. Hull groups Lakalei and Idaté with Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede as Ramelaic. Grimes and Edwards (2026) split off Central Timor first, and put Lakalei and Idaté in Timor-Babar with everything to the east, because they share the loss of an old p.

C1 and C2. The project’s two questions. C1: where does Lakalei-Idate go? C2: is Welaun a dialect of Tetun or a Central Timor language? This page is mostly about C1.

Two kinds of clue

Sound changes point one way, new words the other, and many words say nothing at all.

Two kinds of evidence can show which languages belong together. One is a sound change they share, such as old p becoming h. The other is a new word they share, one that replaced an older word. A word that every language kept, a retention, proves nothing. The words below are real, from the LexiRumah word lists. Hover over or tap a word for its source.

The star. A starred form, like *mata ‘eye’, is a word reconstructed for Proto-Malayo-Polynesian, the ancestor of all these languages.

  • has in the eastern languages

What the data show, suggest and leave open

What the data show

  • The vocabulary trees place Lakalei-Idate beside Central Timor and never with the eastern languages, and outside languages put the root outside that pair, whatever outgroups are used.
  • That placement depends on choices that shouldn’t matter (0 to 98% bootstrap support), and Idaté on its own leans towards its neighbour Habu.
  • Once old words are removed, the shared new words no longer favour Central Timor over Habu.
  • The loss of *p fits Grimes and Edwards’s tree better if a lost sound rarely comes back, and the sound changes never fit the vocabulary tree best.

What they suggest

A history shaped by contact or a dialect chain, a linkage, as well as by descent. Lakalei resembles Mambae, its neighbour 10 km away; Idaté resembles Habu, 5 to 11 km away.

What remains unknown

Whether either classification is historically right. Settling it needs specialists to review the 47 decisive word comparisons, fuller word lists with regular sound correspondences, and an independent rerun of the analyses.

      Where the root falls

      Grimes and Edwards’s tree needs the root on the Central Timor stem. It never falls there: not with any set of outgroups, not without the reconstructed proto-language, not under a relaxed clock, and not when missing words are counted as absent. It moves between the Flores and the Rote-Meto branches depending on those choices.

      That rules out one reconciliation, but it doesn’t prove an inherited subgroup. A rooted group in a word tree only means its members share more words with each other than with the outgroups. It shows common descent only if those shared words are new, inherited and not borrowed.

      The reconstruction as a language. Proto-Malayo-Polynesian is built partly from these languages’ relatives and has no loans or gaps, so it can pull the root towards conservative languages. It is used only as a check.

      Shared words: old and new

      Counted naively, the shared words favour Central Timor. Clean out old words mistaken for new ones and the lead disappears against Habu.

      The triplet test takes three groups and counts the basic concepts on which exactly two of them share a new word (an innovation). Each row below removes another source of error: first old words the automatic rule mistook for new, then words also found in languages outside Timor. The last row codes compound words by their parts, without checking by hand.

      The control. Welaun’s contact with Tetun is documented. The same test finds it in borrowable words and not in stable ones, which is the one contact signal that survives correction for the many tests run.

        Stable concepts, representative lists (E46, E51). Counts are summed over combinations of dialect lists, so they exceed the number of concepts. Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede are Nuclear Central Timor.

        Words shared with only one other group are few. After review, two remain on the Central Timor side, ‘cry’ and ‘spit’, against up to nine on the eastern side that nobody has yet checked by hand. A test of regular sound correspondences in these words found too few in these short lists to tell loans from inheritance.

        Sound changes

        Lakalei and Idaté have lost the old *p, like the eastern languages. Whether that counts depends on whether a lost sound can come back.

        Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede keep *p in 54 of 55 matched words; Lakalei and Idaté in none of 6. Lakalei-Idate has h at the start of a word (*pitu > hitu ‘seven’), loss or h between vowels (*əpat > aːt ‘four’), and w before *u (*hapuy > wai ‘fire’). The eastern languages have h throughout. And Welaun, a Central Timor language on every other count, lost *p too, so the change happened at least twice on Timor.

        Lenition. A weakening of a consonant, like p becoming h or disappearing. The reverse, h becoming p, is almost unknown.

        Explanation for the loss of *pAssessment
        One change in an ancestor shared with the eastern languagesPossible. Lakalei-Idate’s outcomes differ a little from the eastern h, but later changes could explain that.
        Separate changes in different lineagesPossible. Welaun shows it has happened at least twice.
        Central Timor lost *p and then got it backVery unlikely. h > p is almost unknown, and loans couldn’t restore it so regularly.
        The change spread by contact between languages already apartPossible, and these data can’t tell it apart from a shared ancestor.

        The decision rule

        A rule fixed before the second round asked for four things before calling it for Central Timor. Only one came through.

        This is the study’s primary analysis. Grimes and Edwards’s placement would have been reported as supported if Timor-Babar came out as a rooted group, or if the root sat on the Central Timor stem. Neither happened either.

        Holm correction. Eleven topology tests were run in the project. Corrected together, none rejects any placement.

        A direct Bayesian comparison does favour Central Timor (log Bayes factor +11.8). It answers a narrower question: which placement fits better under a model that treats every shared word as inherited. The corrected AU tests ask whether the preference survives resampling the word list, and it doesn’t.

        How it was done

        1. Data. Basic vocabulary from LexiRumah 3.0.1: 70 lects of the Timor region and 152 concepts, with 24 lects, one per language, in the model-based analyses. The Idaté and Lakalei lists come from surveys by Marian Klamer (2002) and Virginia Dawson (2014).
        2. Cognates. Automatic detection with LexStat, scored against expert judgements (B-cubed F 0.81) and checked under five other codings.
        3. Trees. Distance, Bayesian and maximum-likelihood trees; a root from seven outgroups and a relaxed clock, with nine sensitivity runs.
        4. Old and new words. Every cognate set classed as a retention or an innovation, validated against experts, checked by review and against two etymological sources, with an audit trail for every change.
        5. Sound changes. Grimes and Edwards’s tables as an expert reflex atlas, checked against an automatic atlas (94% agreement), scored on the rival trees under different assumptions about reversibility.

        Pre-registered. The protocol was frozen and fingerprinted (SHA-256) on 10 October 2026, before any analysis. Fourteen dated addenda followed, each pushed before the tests it governs.

        Data and code

        Every estimate in the paper’s main text can be rebuilt with one command, bash reproduce.sh principal, and is listed with the file it comes from. The whole pipeline reruns from pinned upstream data.

        derived/audit/
        Every excluded or reclassified form and word set with a reason code; the 47 decisive comparisons, ready for a specialist; every *p reflex with its environment.
        DATA_DICTIONARY.md · PROVENANCE.md
        Every lect, character, cognate assignment and exclusion code; every source with its version and licence.
        derived/ge2026_tables/
        Grimes and Edwards’s 257 comparative tables as data: 14,401 forms, with the authors’ marked reflexes.
        derived/reflex_atlas/
        Reflexes of the Proto-Malayo-Polynesian consonants, language by language, from the expert tables and from automatic alignment.
        PROTOCOL.md
        The frozen protocol and its fourteen addenda.
        SPECIALIST_REVIEW.md · REPLICATION.md
        What a specialist reviewer and an independent replicator are asked to do.

        Liabut is coined from Tetun lia ‘word, speech’ and abut ‘root’: the roots of words.

        What would settle it

        • Historical linguists who know these languages reviewing the 47 decisive word comparisons on both sides.
        • Regular sound correspondences from fuller word lists. Grimes and Edwards’s 494-item lists for Idaté and Lakalei aren’t public.
        • Hull’s 1998 lexical percentages, and Alcantara’s full Idaté corpus.
        • Someone independent rerunning the main analyses.

        Use of AI. The code, the analyses, the drafting and the mock referee report were produced with substantial assistance from Claude (Anthropic), an AI system, under the author’s direction. Every analysis, decision and correction is recorded in the public repository.
