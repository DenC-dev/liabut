Project Liabut

How do Timor’s Austronesian languages branch?

Two classifications of the Austronesian languages of Timor disagree. Geoffrey Hull puts Lakalei and Idaté with Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede. Charles Grimes and Owen Edwards make Mambae, Kemak, Tokodede and Welaun a branch of their own and put Lakalei and Idaté with the languages to the east. Liabut tests both against the basic vocabulary of 24 languages, under a protocol frozen before any analysis.

In brief: Welaun is Central Timor, the eastern groupings find no support in the vocabulary, and Lakalei-Idate is still unresolved. The evidence, and every number behind it, follows.

What the vocabulary says

Two questions divide the classifications. C1: does Lakalei-Idate belong with Central Timor, as in Hull, or with Timor-Babar, as in Grimes and Edwards (2026)? C2: is Welaun a dialect of Tetun, as Hull (1998) first had it, or a Central Timor language, as Edwards (2019) argued?

  • Lakalei-Idate remains unresolved. Every tree analysis places it beside Central Timor (posterior 0.91), and none supports Timor-Babar. Yet a borrowing model cannot exclude Grimes and Edwards’s placement, provided Lakalei-Idate borrowed more than a quarter of its core vocabulary from Central Timor.
  • Welaun belongs with Central Timor. Forcing that group against forbidding it gives a log Bayes factor of +10.3 (posterior 0.86), and Welaun’s Tetun look sits in borrowable words, as contact would leave it.
  • Fabronic, Timor-Babar and Eastern Timor get no lexical support. They score 0 to 3% bootstrap and a posterior of 0.00. Grimes and Edwards define Timor-Babar by sound changes, so this does not refute it; if it is a subgroup, contact has since reshaped its vocabulary.
  • The low-level groups hold. Lakalei-Idate, Kawaimina, Wetar-Atauro, Nuclear Rote, Kisar-Luang and Babar all pass the bar. Galoli pairs with Dadu’a, and Helong falls outside Rote-Meto.
  • The pre-registered rule returned “unresolved” for both questions, because no contested group reached 70% bootstrap support in both distance trees. Everything beyond that rule is labelled post hoc.

Ramelaic. Hull’s group of Mambae, Kemak, Tokodede and Idalaka, his name for Idaté and Lakalei together.

Timor-Babar. Grimes and Edwards’s branch for every Austronesian language of Timor outside Central Timor, defined by the change of *p to *h.

Pre-registered. The protocol was frozen and fingerprinted (SHA-256) on 10 October 2026, before any analysis. Eight dated addenda followed, each pushed before the tests it governs.

Lakalei-Idate: inherited or borrowed?

Lakalei and Idaté, spoken in central Timor-Leste, carry the whole disagreement. Their vocabulary puts them beside Central Timor, while their sound changes look eastern: they have *p lenition and *ŋ > n, and lack Central Timor’s *ŋ > g, k. Five post hoc tests ask whether the Central Timor signal in their vocabulary was inherited or borrowed.

Posterior probability. The share of sampled trees that contain a group. A value of 0.91 means 91% of the trees put Lakalei-Idate next to Central Timor.

Shared replacements, stable and borrowable

The triplet test counts concepts on which exactly two of three groups share a word that replaced the Proto-Malayo-Polynesian one. The largest count marks the pair the tree predicts; a lopsided split of the other two marks contact. Run separately on the 60 hardest-to-borrow concepts of the Leipzig-Jakarta list and on the other 92, it shows where the signal lives.

The check. Welaun’s contact with Tetun is documented. The test finds it (Z = 3.1) and places it in borrowable words (Z = 6.9), not in stable ones (Z = 0.6).

Which half carries the signal

In stable vocabulary, five of the eight Central Timor items are shared by both Idaté and Lakalei, so they predate the split between the two. In borrowable vocabulary, six of eight belong to Lakalei alone and match Mambae, spoken 10 km away: ‘cloud’ sabai, ‘neck’, ‘eat’ mia ~ mua. The counts are small (binomial p = 0.11), but the pattern fits an old inheritance with recent local borrowing on top.

How much borrowing each placement needs

Approximate Bayesian computation simulated 20,000 histories under each placement, letting Lakalei-Idate borrow from the other side, and kept the 1% closest to the 18 observed triplet counts.

Cross-validation. Run on its own simulated data, the method picks the true placement in 87 to 94% of cases, so it can tell the two apart.

The simulation prefers Grimes and Edwards’s placement, but only if Lakalei-Idate borrowed more than a quarter of its core vocabulary from Central Timor, and as much core vocabulary as other vocabulary, unlike Welaun’s known contact. That is above the 20% bar set in advance for a defensible placement. On the full lexical likelihood, the unconstrained tree beats the constrained one by 10.5 log units.

Two further views suggest the tie is old and loose. Historical glottometry finds Lakalei-Idate cohesive on its own (subgroupiness 1.42) and only faintly tied to Central Timor (0.06) or to the east (0.08). Under a strict clock, about 80% of samples still place it with Central Timor, splitting off (relative height 0.29) barely before Central Timor itself diversified (0.27).

The reading that fits most of the evidence is an old, conservative member of a central Timor dialect chain, close to Hull’s Ramelaic, which broke away about when Central Timor diversified and kept ties to the east. The comparative method’s own test, regular sound correspondences across complete wordlists, has not yet been applied to these data.

Subgroupiness. In glottometry, how many innovations a group shares exclusively, weighted by how few cut across it. Ramelaic scores 0.

Sound changes

Grimes and Edwards’s comparative tables were turned into data: 257 tables and 14,401 forms, with the reflexes the authors mark. Eight diagnostic changes were coded from them. Lakalei-Idate’s profile resembles Timor-Babar’s, but two facts weaken the diagnostics: Welaun, Central Timor on every other count, also has *p > h, and *ŋ > n also occurs in Sika, a Flores outgroup. Hull’s own Ramelaic evidence, initial *b- > h, is visible in the same tables: Lakalei-Idate shares it with Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede, and Galoli and Ili’uun share it too.

Adding the sound changes to the vocabulary does not move Lakalei-Idate east. With eight changes it groups with Central Timor at 80% (ultrafast bootstrap), and Timor-Babar gets 0 in every combination. Support does depend on how cognates are coded, from 92% with SCA cognates down to 15 to 20% at two LexStat thresholds, where Lakalei-Idate still groups with Mambae, Kemak and Tokodede.

Reflex. The sound a language has where the proto-language had a given sound, as Tetun h where Proto-Malayo-Polynesian had *p.

How it was done

  1. Data. Basic vocabulary from LexiRumah 3.0.1: 70 lects of the Timor region and 152 concepts, with 24 lects, one per language, in the model-based analyses. ABVD’s expert cognate judgements served as a benchmark.
  2. Cognates. Automatic detection with LexStat in LingPy, scored against the experts at a B-cubed F of 0.81. A gate of known low-level groups had to be recovered before any contested group was scored.
  3. Trees. Distance trees with 1,000 concept bootstraps, Bayesian trees in MrBayes with Bayes factors from stepping-stone sampling, and maximum-likelihood trees in IQ-TREE.
  4. Sound changes. Grimes and Edwards’s tables as an expert reflex atlas, checked against an automatic atlas (94% agreement), and coded as characters alongside the vocabulary.
  5. Inheritance or contact. Retention and innovation classes for every cognate set, the triplet test, a split test, approximate Bayesian computation, historical glottometry and a strict-clock analysis.

B-cubed F. A score from 0 to 1 for how well automatic cognate sets match expert ones. 0.81 is usable but below the 0.89 that List, Greenhill and Gray (2017) report across families, which is why the results are checked under five codings.

The pre-registration trail

  1. 10 October 2026Protocol v1 frozen and fingerprinted before any analysis, with the decision rule: a contested group needs 70% bootstrap support in both distance trees, on the primary data and two screened variants.
  2. 10 OctoberAddenda 1 and 2. The gate of known groups first failed on one group. A revised gate was adopted (post hoc), and further rules were fixed before any contested group was scored.
  3. 10 OctoberPre-registered tests run. Decision: unresolved for C1 and C2.
  4. 10 to 11 OctoberAddenda 3 and 4 (post hoc). Bayesian reruns, Grimes and Edwards’s tables as data, reflex atlases and total-evidence trees.
  5. 11 OctoberAddenda 5 and 6 (post hoc). Hull’s *b and *k as characters, an ASJP check, cognate-coding robustness and Alcantara’s (2015) Idaté list.
  6. 11 OctoberAddenda 7 and 8 (post hoc). Shared innovations, the triplet test, contact-aware trees, the split test, borrowing simulation, glottometry, clock dating and other branches.

Limits

Most results after the frozen protocol are post hoc, and about twenty analyses were run without correction for multiple testing. The cognates are automatic and have not been checked by a specialist, and the C1 result depends on how they are coded. LexiRumah and Grimes and Edwards share sources and an editor. The sound-change characters come from illustrative tables and are thin for Idaté and Lakalei. The triplet test assumes similar rates of change across lineages, which the tree does not show, and the simulation reduces the data to 18 counts. Hull (1998) and Carvalho (2001) are cited from secondary sources.

What would settle C1: expert cognate judgements for the decisive concepts, Grimes and Edwards’s full field lists for Idaté and Lakalei, Alcantara’s complete Idaté corpus, and a regular-correspondence analysis of complete wordlists.

Use of AI. The code, the analyses and the drafting were carried out with substantial assistance from Claude (Anthropic), an AI system, under the author’s direction. Every analysis, decision and correction is recorded in the public repository so that it can be checked.
